About the 34th General Chapter

The 34th General Chapter of the Congregation of SJSM was held at Saint-Marc in France from May 01 to May 13, 2024. There were 40 delegated sisters representing the Indian, German, French, Ukraine, USA, Philippines, and Italian regions.

"34th General Chapter of SJSM Concludes in France with New Leadership and Renewed Mission"

The 34th General Chapter of the Congregation of the Sisters of Saint Joseph of Saint-Marc (SJSM) took place at Saint-Marc in France from May 01 to May 13, 2024. This important event saw the participation of forty delegated sisters, representing two Indian Provinces, the German Province, the French Province, the Ukraine Region, the USA, the Philippines, and Italy. The chapter focused on discussing the Congregation's future directions, evaluating past achievements, and setting new goals for their mission..

The Chapter was a time of deep reflection, discussion, and decision-making, focused on the future directions and leadership of the Congregation. It was during this Chapter that Sr. Shobha Stanly was elected as the new Superior General of the Congregation. Her election marks a new chapter in the leadership of the Congregation, promising continuity and new initiatives in their mission and ministry.

"New Leadership and Global Representation Mark 34th General Chapter of SJSM"

the General Council was formed with the election of four General Councilors who will assist Sr. Shobha Stanly in her duties. These Councilors include Sr. Marie Therese Frie from the German Province, who brings a wealth of experience from Europe; Sr. Mary Kannampuzha from the Sanjo Province in Vengoor, representing the Indian subcontinent with its rich cultural heritage; Sr. Daniele Vetter from the French Province, who provides a deep connection to the Congregation’s roots; and Sr. Ligy Payyappilly from the Ukraine Region, offering perspectives from a region facing significant challenges and transformations.

The presence and participation of these sisters from various parts of the world underscore the Congregation's commitment to global solidarity, unity in diversity, and shared mission. The discussions and resolutions from this General Chapter are expected to inspire and guide the Congregation’s activities and spiritual life in the coming years, ensuring they continue to live out their charism of service and devotion to the teachings of Saint Joseph and their founding principles. .

INFORMATION FROM THE GENERALATE – 2025

Spiritual Reflection & Core Message

The year 2025, with all its "ups and downs, moments of joys, sorrows, stress, good health and illness," brings us to the profound realization of St. Teresa of Avila: **"God alone suffices!"** This truth guides us through all moments, bright and challenging, toward God's "majestic mercy and love."
--- Generalate Missions and Community Life in 2025 The year 2025 was marked by extensive global travel and significant spiritual and administrative milestones for the Sisters of St. Joseph of Saint-Marc. **Global Outreach:** Superior General Sr. Shobha Stanly and General Councilor Sr. Ligy Payyappilly spent three months (Jan 2 – Mar 28) on a massive visitation across **India**, reaching 36 communities in both provinces and witnessing the Final Commitments of several sisters. The Generalate also expanded its roots as Sr. Danièle Vetter and Sr. Sheeba Thomas traveled to **Madagascar** in March to bless and inaugurate the Congregation's first convent there. Later in the year (Aug 5 – 20), Sr. Marie-Therese Frie and Sr. Ligy Payyappilly made their first visit to the thriving communities in the **Philippines**. **Congregational Leadership:** A pivotal moment occurred in May when Sr. Shobha Stanly attended the **UISG Conference** in **Rome**, an important gathering of Superiors General. During her visit, she witnessed the historic election of the **New Pope Leo XIV** on May 8th, confirming the vibrant faith of the global Church. Administratively, the **European Councils’ Meeting** was held in Saint-Marc (May 22 – 23) to address pressing issues in European religious life. **Spiritual and Administrative Milestones:** The year included joyful celebrations like the **Final Commitment of Sr. Tavika in Ukraine** (Sept 6) and the visit of Archbishop Stephan Burger to the St. Trudpert Provincial House in November for the Feast of Christ the King. Personally, there was joy as Sr. Mary Kannampuzha successfully earned her **European driving license** (Oct 27). The Congregation also celebrated the release of Sr. Ligy Payyappilly's book, **'Called to Ukraine,'** in January, and her two weeks dedicated to preaching the Scriptures in **Spain** in September, a marvelous contribution to spreading the Good News.

Sr. Shobha Stanly,
Superior General SJSM

A Road Not Chosen by Chance: The Testimony of Sisters Ligy and Laura from Ukraine's Frontline Territories

There are journeys after which a person can never look at the world the same way again. Such was the trip of Sister Ligy and Sister Laura through central, eastern, and southern Ukraine — Kyiv, Kharkiv, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson. They did not go as observers, but as witnesses — to see, to hear, to stand beside people for whom war has long stopped being news and become a daily way of surviving.

The Zero Line

The sisters were at what are called "zero positions" — places as close to the frontline as it is possible to be, where the distance between life and death is measured not in kilometers but in seconds. There are no pauses between air raid alarms: shelling struck every hour, and every hour became a trial — not only physical, but above all spiritual. Living at such a place, where danger leaves no room for rest even at night, is an experience that cannot be fully conveyed in words to those who have not lived through it.

Kherson: A City on the Edge

The impression left by Kherson was especially heavy. Nearly every building in the city has been destroyed — the war has left its mark on every street. Part of the city is still under occupation, and even the liberated part has found no peace: there is no water. The cause is a tragedy that shocked the whole world: the destruction of the dam, after which many people died, and bodies still remain in the water. Because of this, the city's water supply is unusable — water has become a source not of life, but of danger.

This is one of the most painful paradoxes of war: liberation does not always mean a return to normal life. People regain freedom of movement but lose the most basic things — clean water, safe housing, the sense that tomorrow will resemble today.

The situation in the occupied part of the city is a separate, even deeper wound. The people who remain there are living through something that, in the 21st century, seems as though it would not be possible: the occupying authorities do not allow humanitarian aid to reach residents, and Russia itself provides none either. To survive, people are eating rats. For five years now, they have had neither electricity nor water — conditions we take for granted as basic have become an unattainable luxury there.

Those Who Survived Captivity

The sisters spoke with women and men who had survived captivity. Such conversations demand particular gentleness and patience — the trauma of captivity stays in the body and memory for a long time, and every account costs the person telling it dearly. That these people found the courage to speak is, in itself, an act of bravery.

Humiliation as a Weapon

One of the men the sisters spoke with shared a story that reveals another dimension of this war — the use of humiliation as a tool of pressure. He recounted that Russian soldiers stopped the bus he was driving and forced every passenger to speak only Russian. Then everyone was taken outside and forced to strip naked in the street.

This is not simply an act of cruelty — it is testimony to how occupation tries to break a person not only physically but from within, stripping away the most fundamental things: the right to one's own language, one's own body, one's own dignity.

The Woman Who Carried Her Life in Her Hands

Among the stories that left the deepest mark on the sisters is that of a woman who looks about ninety years old, though she is in fact fifty-seven. The gap between her outward appearance and her biological age is silent testimony to what the war has done to her. She suffered a lacerating wound from a missile explosion and, holding her own abdominal organs in her hands, walked to a doctor on her own.

It is hard to find words adequate to such an experience. This is a story not only of physical suffering, but of a will strong enough to keep a body moving even when moving seems impossible.

Children Given Back the Chance to Learn

Among the people the sisters met is a family with two children, eleven and thirteen years old, who have serious injuries. In the place where they currently live, schooling is impossible: there is neither safety nor the conditions for it. Hearing this story, we all decided together to give these children an opportunity, and we invited the family to move to us — to give them what they deserve: the chance to learn, to grow, to have something resembling a childhood after all they have endured.

This decision is not simply an act of mercy. It is a recognition that a child's education and future should not depend on where exactly the front line happens to fall.

A City Where Every Step Matters

One of the most telling details of this journey is a warning the sisters heard and carried with them from their own experience: in the city, one must walk very carefully, because mines are hidden everywhere. ‘Said matter-of-fact’, this sentence in fact describes a completely transformed reality: a space that was once an ordinary city of sidewalks, courtyards, and parks now demands constant vigilance. Even an ordinary walk becomes a decision requiring a calculation of risk.

What to Do With What Was Seen

Coming home did not put a period on this story — on the contrary, it has become its continuation. Having seen with their own eyes how people live practically at zero, Sister Ligy and Sister Laura — and, together with them, all of us who prayed and waited for their return — understood that simply telling what they had seen was not enough.

So a decision was made: to direct all our shared efforts toward bringing in donors to help build homes for families who still live practically on the frontline and have no way to move to safer territory — to us. These are people who, for various reasons, cannot leave: some have nowhere to go, others have nothing to go with, still others are simply so exhausted that they no longer have the strength for another move into the unknown.

We recognize that we, too, sometimes live through shelling, and that this leaves its own mark. But comparing our experience to what is happening at zero, in a ruined Kherson, or in the places from which people emerge from captivity, is impossible. The difference is too great to consider our trials equivalent. That is exactly why our duty is not to stay silent about what we have seen, but to act: to seek out donors, to build homes, to give families a real chance to move to a place where shelling every hour is not part of daily life.

Every home built is not just a roof overhead. It is the chance for a child to go back to school, for parents to stop counting the minutes between air raid alarms, for a family to begin living, rather than merely surviving.

The account of Sister Ligy and Sister Laura is not simply a description of ruined buildings or a tally of shelling. It is testimony about people: about a woman who walked to a doctor holding her own life in her hands; about children held back a chance to learn; about the residents of Kherson living without water among the ruins; about those who returned from captivity and found the courage to speak; about a man who was forced to strip naked in the street simply for wanting to speak his own language.

Journeys like this are a reminder: behind every piece of news about the war stand real faces, real names, real fates. And often the most we can do is to see, to listen, not to look away — and to turn what we have heard into concrete action that gives families a roof overhead and the chance to keep living.

We ask all the sisters for prayer for the people we met, for the families still living at zero, for the children now given the chance to learn. And if it is on anyone's heart, we also ask you to join in the search for donors for this effort, so that together we may build more homes and give more families a chance at a safe life.

With love and prayer,
Sister Kristina and the sisters from Ukraine

Mission in Madagascar


Mission to Madagascar: Assessing Needs and Building Community Connections

From October 11th to 18th, Sr. Sheeba Thomas, Sr. Chimene, and Sr. Shobha Stanly embarked on a mission to Madagascar, coinciding with their Congregation's Founder’s week. Despite initial challenges, including Sr. Shobha's lost passport, their journey proceeded with divine assistance. Upon arriving in Antananarivo, they were warmly received by Sr. Miriam of the Sacred Heart of Ragusa. Their mission took them to various cities, including Diego and Daraina, where they were met with enthusiastic welcomes, singing, and dancing from local communities. They explored the potential for establishing a new convent, supported by the local Bishop Marc Benjamin and Mr. Merenti, who offered land for this purpose. In Vohemar, they witnessed the vibrant faith of over 1000 students and villagers, culminating in an Adoration of the Blessed Sacrament. The delegation also participated in a Holy Eucharist and Confirmation ceremony for 62 children and adults. Throughout their visit, they observed the natural beauty and rich resources of Madagascar, alongside the significant social and economic challenges faced by the local population. This mission underscored their commitment to helping the people of Madagascar achieve development and progress while encouraging the sisters to respond to the Holy Spirit’s call to address contemporary issues with renewed dedication.

Communities in the Philippines: A Growing Presence Since 2008





In 2008, the Congregation of SJSM expanded its mission and established communities in the Philippines, marking a significant milestone in our journey. The decision to extend our reach to this vibrant and diverse country was driven by a vision to share our Charism of Adoration and Service, rooted in Eucharistic Spirituality, with more people. Currently, we have two flourishing communities located on different islands. One community is situated in Cavite City, which is in close proximity to the bustling capital, Manila. Cavite City offers a dynamic environment where our sisters engage in various ministries, reaching out to the local population with compassion and dedication. This community has become a beacon of hope and spiritual enrichment for many residents. The other community is located in San Carlos, another significant area within the Philippines. This community also plays a crucial role in the spiritual and social development of the region, fostering a sense of belonging and support among the local people. The sisters here work tirelessly to embody the values of our Congregation, making a tangible difference in the lives they touch. One of the most inspiring aspects of our mission in the Philippines is the growing interest among young girls to join our Congregation. Many are drawn to our way of life, motivated by the desire to live out the Charism of Adoration and Service. These young aspirants are eager to embrace the Eucharistic Spirituality that defines our Congregation, committing themselves to a life of prayer, service, and community living. The establishment of these communities has not only broadened our geographical reach but also enriched our Congregation with the cultural diversity and vibrant spirit of the Filipino people. As we continue to nurture and support these communities, we remain committed to our mission of spreading love, faith, and service across the Philippines and beyond.
In summary, our communities in the Philippines are thriving centers of spiritual growth and service. The dedication of our sisters and the enthusiastic response from young aspirants highlight the impactful presence of our Congregation in this beautiful country. We look forward to further growth and deeper engagement with the Filipino community, fostering a future filled with hope and faith.

A Visit to the United States of America

From October 18 to November 12, 2024, I traveled with Sr. Mary Kannampuzha to the USA. After receiving my visa at the last minute, we flew from Frankfurt to Corpus Christi, Texas. The temperature there was over 30°C, much warmer than France, but our sisters warmly welcomed us and took great care of us. The Corpus Christi community has three sisters who work in a hospital.

We then visited San Antonio, where we celebrated the 150th anniversary of the Archdiocese and met Archbishop Gustavo Garcia-Siller. In New York, we visited communities in the Diocese of Brooklyn and explored some iconic places. We also traveled to North Carolina, Ohio, and Pennsylvania, meeting our elderly sisters, including Mother Raphael, who, at 99, still serves with joy and dedication.

This trip was filled with love and hospitality. Our sisters in the USA live simply, embodying our charism and serving with faith and devotion. I thank God and all the sisters who made this journey a memorable and inspiring experience, showing that the SJSM spirit is alive and well in the United States.

Sr. Shobha Stanly,
Superior General SJSM
